## Abstract

Constrained Delta Evolution (CDE) permits state changes generated by heterogeneous Delta operators and explored through binary trees, adaptive multiway trees, directed acyclic graphs, or cyclic state graphs. This flexibility introduces a critical semantic distinction: **repeating a Delta**, **repeating a decision about a Delta**, and **revisiting a state** are different events. In constructive BTP-style search, a candidate decision must ordinarily be resolved at most once along a path, regardless of whether its Yes or No branch is taken. A No branch is therefore not an absence of history: it records a meaningful exclusion. Yet the same rule cannot be imposed universally on reconfiguration, iterative optimization, stochastic exploration, or feedback-driven recursive evolution, where revisiting states or reapplying operations may be legitimate. This paper defines typed decision records, scoped Delta identities, path-local memory, exclusion semantics, state equivalence, loop-back policies, and termination contracts. It provides counterexamples, proof obligations, a reference exploration procedure, and a conformance-oriented validation plan. It distinguishes guaranteed properties under explicit assumptions from heuristic policies and open research hypotheses.

**Keywords:** CDE; path memory; NoSubtree; YesSubtree; Delta identity; decision uniqueness; loop back; cycle detection; state equivalence; termination; completeness; structural relay

## 1. Why Path Memory Is a Foundational Issue

The transition from Bucket Tree of Permutation (BTP) to Dynamic BTP (D-BTP) and then CDE expands the representation of both problems and solvers. A binary assignment tree is only one possible search topology. A CDE state may generate `M` candidate Deltas and realize `N` outgoing transition edges, with `M` and `N` varying independently. A branch records the Delta and its transition provenance, but **an edge label alone is not a complete search history**.

Consider an assignment candidate `Assign(A, J)`. If the search accepts this decision, the candidate must not be reconsidered as an undecided option further down the same constructive path. Less obviously, if the search rejects it, that rejection must also remain visible. Otherwise a later generator may propose `Assign(A, J)` again, causing duplicate subtrees or even nontermination. The original Yes/No intuition is therefore a path-level **decision-resolution** invariant, not merely a rule against repeated execution.

At the same time, a routing optimizer may legitimately remove a passenger from one route and later reassign that passenger. A game search may revisit a board position under a different history. A learning system may retry a previously unsuccessful operation after obtaining new evidence. Treating all of these as forbidden repetitions would exclude valid computations.

CDE must make the memory and loop policies explicit, composable, and independently testable.

## 2. Minimal Formal Context

Let a CDE exploration configuration be

\[
q_t=(s_t,c_t,e_t,h_t,p_t,b_t),
\]

where:

- \(s_t\) is the domain state or logical State Container;
- \(c_t\) is the applicable constraint environment and its version;
- \(e_t\) is the available evidence or belief context;
- \(h_t\) is the path decision and transition history;
- \(p_t\) is the active generator, solver, and exploration policy;
- \(b_t\) is the remaining budget or other termination-relevant resource.

A candidate Delta \(\delta\) is generated by \(G(q_t)\). A transition relation

\[
q_t\xrightarrow{(\delta,a,o)}q_{t+1}
\]

records the candidate, its disposition \(a\), and an outcome \(o\). An outcome may be deterministic, nondeterministic, or observation-dependent. A decision to exclude a candidate may change only the decision history, not the domain state.

**Modeling rule:** do not assume that every decision creates a new domain state or that every domain-state change corresponds to a unique decision.

### 2.1 Three distinct identities

Define separately:

1. **Delta identity** \(I_\Delta(\delta,\kappa)\): whether two proposals express the same operation under a specified identity scope \(\kappa\).
2. **Decision identity** \(I_D(\delta,a,\kappa)\): whether a candidate decision has already been resolved in the relevant scope, independently of its chosen disposition.
3. **State identity** \(I_S(q)\): whether two configurations are equivalent for a particular downstream purpose.

These keys are not interchangeable. Canonical serialization, stable identifiers, and context-version rules belong in the contract rather than in ad hoc solver code.

## 3. YesSubtree and NoSubtree: Both Are Decisions

In a constructive binary BTP profile, the branch alternatives for an unresolved candidate \(\delta\) are:

\[
\operatorname{Yes}(\delta):\text{ apply or commit }\delta,
\qquad
\operatorname{No}(\delta):\text{ exclude }\delta.
\]

The Yes branch records a commitment; the No branch records an exclusion. A No branch is **not** the same as “the generator did not produce this candidate” or “the scheduler did not select it yet.”

Let \(A_t\) be the set of accepted candidate identities and \(R_t\) the set of explicitly rejected candidate identities along a path. Define the resolved-decision set

\[
U_t=A_t\cup R_t.
\]

Under a fixed-context, one-shot constructive profile, the eligibility invariant is

\[
I_\Delta(\delta,\kappa)\notin U_t.
\]

For either Yes or No, the chosen identity is added to \(U_{t+1}\). If the same candidate is generated again, the solver must recognize that its decision is already resolved rather than expand it as a fresh branch.

### 3.1 A concrete NoSubtree counterexample

Suppose the candidate universe is \(D=\{d_1,d_2\}\). The root considers \(d_1\), and its No child records only the unchanged domain state. If the generator depends only on that domain state, it may propose \(d_1\) again. The search can produce an infinite chain

```text
root: decide d1
  No(d1): domain state unchanged
    decide d1
      No(d1): domain state unchanged
        decide d1
          ...
```

The defect disappears when `Exclude(d1)` is recorded as path-local decision history and the generator or eligibility filter respects it.

### 3.2 Decision history is not execution history

A rejected assignment was never executed. An accepted assignment may be executed once, rolled back, or superseded. Thus an execution log cannot substitute for a decision log. Likewise, a path decision log is not automatically a complete audit trail of external effects.

## 4. Four Rejection and Nonselection Semantics

A general CDE system must distinguish at least the following dispositions:

| Disposition | Meaning | Typical persistence | Creates a new domain state? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `EXCLUDE` | Candidate is explicitly ruled out within a stated scope | Path- or context-scoped | Usually no |
| `INFEASIBLE` | Candidate violates a currently applicable hard constraint | Until relevant constraint/context changes | Usually no |
| `DEFER` | Candidate is not explored now | Scheduler-dependent | No |
| `PRUNE` | A search region is discarded under a bound, proof, or heuristic | Depends on validity of pruning reason | Not necessarily |

A fifth disposition, `APPLY`, commits a Delta and produces one or more outcomes. `OBSERVE` may update evidence without committing a domain action.

**Critical distinction:** `DEFER` must not silently become `EXCLUDE`. Similarly, a heuristic `PRUNE` must not be represented as a proven infeasibility claim.

### 4.1 Scope of exclusion

A rejection record should include at least:

```text
DecisionRecord:
  delta_key
  disposition
  decision_scope
  context_fingerprint
  reason_code
  evidence_or_proof_reference
  policy_version
  sequence_or_parent_reference
```

Possible scopes include `PATH`, `STATE_CONTEXT`, `SUBPROBLEM`, and `GLOBAL`. A global exclusion is justified only when it is valid for every affected configuration. Context-sensitive exclusions require an explicit invalidation or revalidation rule.

## 5. Delta Uniqueness Is Not Decision Uniqueness

A Delta is an operation description, such as `Assign(A,J)`, `Swap(i,j)`, or `AdjustWeight(w,+0.1)`. A decision is the resolution of a proposal in a particular context. A transition is an observed or computed state change.

These differ in several ways:

- Two syntactically different Deltas may be semantically equivalent.
- The same syntactic Delta may have different effects in different states.
- A single Delta may yield multiple stochastic outcomes.
- A decision to reject a Delta may produce no domain-state transition.
- The same Delta may legitimately be applied more than once in an iterative or reconfigurable solver.

For a fixed finite constructive candidate universe \(D\), canonical identity may be defined simply by the normalized operation and operands. For an adaptive system, identity may require the relevant target, state context, constraint scope, and semantic version.

Do **not** add an arbitrary incrementing version number merely to evade a repetition rule. Versions must reflect a genuine change in semantics or future-relevant context.

## 6. Path-Local Memory Versus State-Level Memory

Two forms of memory serve different purposes.

**Path-local memory** describes what has happened or been decided on one exploration trajectory. It is needed for one-shot decisions, history-dependent constraints, and proof of progress.

**State-level memory** indexes configurations or subproblems already explored, perhaps by many paths or workers. It supports transposition tables, dynamic programming, duplicate detection, and shared bounds.

A compact representation is

\[
H_t=(A_t,R_t,L_t),
\]

where \(L_t\) is an ordered log or sufficient summary of history-sensitive events. A state cache uses a separate key

\[
K_t=\operatorname{Canonicalize}(s_t,c_t,e_t,h_t^{\mathrm{relevant}},p_t^{\mathrm{relevant}},b_t^{\mathrm{relevant}}).
\]

Only fields that affect legal future behavior, attainable outcomes, or the correctness of cached values need to appear in the equivalence key. However, omitting such a field makes merging unsound. Budget may be excluded from a cache key only if the cached result is valid independently of that budget, or if a sound dominance relation is used.

### 6.1 Same domain state, different futures

Two paths may reach the same assignment set while carrying different exclusions. Their next legal moves can differ. Merging them by assignment set alone is unsafe.

Conversely, two paths may have different histories but identical future-relevant feasible actions and objective consequences. Under a proved equivalence relation, they may be merged into one DAG node without losing the relevant answer.

## 7. Loop Back: Three Different Phenomena

“Loop Back” should not be treated as one undifferentiated event.

1. **Repeated Delta:** the same operation is proposed or executed again.
2. **Repeated decision:** the same scoped candidate is resolved again as if undecided.
3. **Repeated state:** a state or equivalent search configuration is revisited, possibly through different operations.

Consider:

\[
S_0\xrightarrow{\delta_a}S_1\xrightarrow{\delta_b}S_0.
\]

No Delta repeats, yet the state does. Thus a no-repeated-Delta rule does not establish acyclicity.

In a reversible scheduling problem, such a loop may be legal but wasteful. In a game or stochastic control problem, revisitation may be essential. In an asynchronous distributed search, apparent repetition may arise from delayed information or independently generated equivalent tasks.

### 7.1 Loop-back policies

| Policy | Behavior | Appropriate use | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| `FORBID_REVISIT` | Reject equivalent previously visited configurations | Finite acyclic search | May eliminate valid solutions if equivalence is too coarse |
| `ALLOW_WITH_BUDGET` | Permit revisits within a step/time/energy bound | Reconfiguration, stochastic search | No completeness beyond budget |
| `ALLOW_IF_DOMINATING` | Revisit only if new label improves a sound dominance criterion | Shortest paths, cost-sensitive DP | Unsound dominance can prune optimum |
| `ALLOW_ON_CONTEXT_CHANGE` | Revisit after material evidence/constraint/policy change | Adaptive search and RSI | Version churn and repeated work |
| `CYCLE_AWARE_POLICY` | Explicitly reason over cycles and long-run outcomes | Games, MDPs, fixed-point methods | Requires specialized algorithms |

These are solver policies, not universal properties of CDE-Core.

## 8. Four CDE Execution Profiles

A useful taxonomy separates model expressiveness from execution restrictions.

### 8.1 Constructive CDE

A finite, fixed candidate universe is progressively resolved or committed. Each candidate may be decided at most once per path. A strict progress measure is the number of unresolved candidates. This profile includes classic Yes/No BTP-style enumeration.

### 8.2 Reconfigurable CDE

Deltas may undo, swap, repair, or revise previous commitments. Candidate decisions and state identities must be scoped to the current configuration. Repeated operations can be legal; cycle detection or budgets control redundant wandering.

### 8.3 Cyclic-State CDE

The domain has legitimate recurrent states. A solver may use graph search, dynamic programming over recurrent classes, value iteration, or other cycle-aware methods. The solution may be a policy or fixed point rather than a terminal leaf.

### 8.4 Recursive / Adaptive CDE

Generators, metrics, constraints, and policies may evolve. The exploration state includes the versions and evidence required for valid interpretation. Previously rejected operations can be reconsidered only under an explicit change-of-context contract, not by accidental history erasure.

A system may combine these profiles across layers—for example, constructive assignment search within a reconfigurable outer optimization loop.

## 9. Termination: What Can Actually Be Guaranteed?

### 9.1 Finite constructive decision theorem

**Proposition 1 (conditional finite-depth termination).** Let \(D\) be a fixed finite set of \(m\) canonical candidate decisions. Assume:

1. Every nonterminal expansion resolves at least one previously unresolved member of \(D\).
2. Resolved decisions are never removed from path memory within that search profile.
3. No transition introduces new candidate identities outside \(D\).
4. Each expansion has finite fan-out, and processing of an individual node terminates.

Then every root-to-leaf exploration path has at most \(m\) decision-resolving expansions. The fully materialized search tree is finite.

**Proof sketch.** Define the natural-number ranking function

\[
\rho(q)=m-|U(q)|.
\]

Each nonterminal expansion strictly decreases \(\rho\) by at least one, while \(\rho\geq0\). Hence no path has more than \(m\) such expansions. Finite branching and finite depth imply a finite tree. \(\square\)

**Limits.** This result does not apply when a node may repeatedly defer without resolving a decision, when new candidate identities are generated indefinitely, or when execution of a node itself fails to terminate. It also does not establish that every feasible solution is represented or found.

### 9.2 More general well-founded progress

A nonconstructive CDE solver may terminate if each relevant transition decreases a well-founded ranking \(\rho(q)\) over a set with no infinite descending chains. A lexicographic ranking can handle several phases, provided the decrease is proved for every transition covered by the claim.

An explicit finite step budget ensures bounded execution, but **budget exhaustion is not proof of convergence, completeness, or optimality**.

### 9.3 Convergence is not finite termination

An iterative process may satisfy \(d(s_t,s^*)\to0\) while never reaching \(s^*\) in finitely many steps. A numerical tolerance, fixed-point criterion, or convergence theorem must be specified separately. Cyclic stochastic systems may require almost-sure termination, expected hitting-time bounds, or other probabilistic notions.

## 10. Completeness, Sound Pruning, and Fairness

A complete solver must explore enough of the admissible space to find a solution whenever one exists under its stated assumptions. A valid state representation alone does not provide completeness.

**Constructive completeness** requires that the candidate universe and branching decisions cover all relevant feasible solutions, that no valid solution is eliminated by constraint propagation, and that traversal eventually explores all necessary branches.

**Graph-search completeness** additionally depends on state equivalence, revisit policy, and the graph's size or applicable fairness assumptions.

**Optimization correctness** requires objective comparability and sound pruning or dominance rules. In branch-and-bound, pruning based on a bound is safe only when the bound has the required mathematical direction and validity for the entire pruned region.

**Fairness** matters for infinite or dynamically generated spaces: a policy that indefinitely prioritizes one region can starve another. Fair scheduling may help establish eventual exploration under specified conditions, but does not guarantee tractable search.

## 11. M-to-N Branching and Memory Propagation

Suppose a parent configuration generates \(M\) candidate Deltas. After filtering and selection, \(N\) transition edges are realized. Each child edge retains:

```text
TransitionEdge:
  parent_configuration_ref
  delta_key
  disposition
  outcome_ref
  constraint_context_ref
  provenance_ref
  child_configuration_ref
```

Each child configuration receives an appropriately scoped decision-memory update. Sibling paths must not accidentally share mutable path-local memory. In particular, the exclusion chosen in one sibling's No branch must not silently exclude the candidate from its Yes sibling or unrelated workers.

A Delta that has multiple nondeterministic outcomes can produce several edges with the same Delta key and disposition but distinct outcome references. That is not necessarily a violation of one-shot decision semantics: the decision can be resolved once, followed by several possible outcomes.

## 12. Reference Exploration Procedure

The following pseudocode is illustrative. It is **not** a universal CDE algorithm; it describes a constructive decision profile with explicit revisit control.

```text
Explore(configuration q, path_memory H, state_cache V):
    if Terminal(q):
        EmitResult(q, H)
        return

    if BudgetExceeded(q):
        EmitIncomplete(q, reason="budget")
        return

    key = FutureRelevantStateKey(q, H)
    if RevisitPolicyRejects(key, q, V):
        RecordSkip(key, reason="revisit-policy")
        return
    UpdateStateCache(V, key, q)

    candidates = GenerateCandidateDeltas(q)
    eligible = []
    for delta in candidates:
        dkey = CanonicalDeltaKey(delta, q.context)
        if ResolvedWithinScope(dkey, H):
            RecordSkip(dkey, reason="already-decided")
            continue
        eligible.append((dkey, delta))

    selected = BranchPolicy(eligible, q.budget, q.evidence)
    for (dkey, delta) in selected:
        # APPLY and EXCLUDE are distinct resolutions.
        for disposition in AllowedDecisions(delta, q):
            H_child = CopyOnWrite(H)
            H_child.add(DecisionRecord(dkey, disposition, q.scope))

            outcomes = TransitionOrExclusion(q, delta, disposition)
            for outcome in outcomes:
                if not HardConstraintsSatisfied(outcome):
                    RecordInvalid(outcome)
                    continue
                child = BuildChildConfiguration(q, outcome, H_child)
                EmitEdge(q, delta, disposition, outcome, child)
                Explore(child, H_child, V)
```

**Important implementation notes:**

- `BranchPolicy` may select only a subset of candidates. If omitted candidates are not retained for later exploration, completeness may be lost.
- `AllowedDecisions` depends on the problem: a general multiway CDE expansion need not produce binary Apply/Exclude alternatives for every Delta.
- The cache must distinguish a node's discovery from completed exploration; otherwise an early cache insertion can incorrectly suppress work in recursive or asynchronous search.
- A shared cache requires concurrency control and appropriate ownership/version semantics.
- A context change may invalidate prior exclusions or cached results, but only under a documented rule.

## 13. Distributed CDE: Memory Is Not One Global Set

Distributed exploration adds another distinction: **path-local decisions are not globally binding facts**. If Worker A excludes a candidate in one branch, Worker B may still be required to explore that candidate in a sibling branch. Broadcasting a path-local exclusion as a global constraint would be a correctness defect.

Distributed workers should exchange typed artifacts:

- **Path records:** decisions valid for a particular branch lineage.
- **Global facts:** validated constraints or proofs whose scope truly spans workers.
- **Search results:** candidates with their state, metric, and constraint versions.
- **Dominance or bound records:** only with a sound scope and validity certificate.
- **Method evidence:** generator improvements, failure patterns, and heuristic observations that do not masquerade as hard constraints.

A worker task should carry a state snapshot or reconstructible reference, Delta scope, path-memory snapshot, constraint and policy versions, budget, and result contract. Task identifiers and deduplication keys should support retries without treating repeated execution as a new logical discovery.

### 13.1 Asynchronous update hazards

An incoming global bound may safely prune work only if the objective and bound semantics match. A newly learned heuristic may reorder work but should not invalidate prior proofs. A new constraint version may require revalidation of cached states and exclusions. Stale worker results remain useful as evidence if labeled correctly, but must not be silently treated as current valid solutions.

## 14. SIF Localization and Structural Relay

Structural Intelligence Framework (SIF) treats localization and addressing as a central functional concern. CDE may provide a complementary **exploratory localization** mechanism: instead of only retrieving an already indexed target, it can generate constrained transformations that progressively identify promising states, candidate capabilities, methods, or structural neighborhoods.

Path memory contributes to this process by preventing accidental rediscovery of already resolved decisions and by preserving negative evidence. Loop-back control allows the system to distinguish unproductive revisits from meaningful re-examination after new evidence.

A useful relay artifact is not simply “this path failed,” but:

```text
ExplorationEvidence:
  starting_context
  delta_sequence_or_compact_path_proof
  accepted_and_excluded_decisions
  observed_outcomes
  constraint_or_metric_versions
  failure_or_success_reason
  transfer_scope
  verification_status
```

Such artifacts may be transferred across domains only when the receiving system can interpret their semantics. The claimed benefits to SIF localization and Collective Learning are research hypotheses requiring domain-specific evaluation.

## 15. Counterexamples and Failure Modes

### 15.1 No-branch amnesia

**Failure:** `Exclude(d)` leaves the domain state unchanged; the generator repeatedly proposes `d`.  
**Correction:** preserve scoped decision resolution in path memory.

### 15.2 Delta uniqueness mistaken for acyclicity

**Failure:** `MoveRight` followed by `MoveLeft` returns to the same state without repeating a Delta.  
**Correction:** maintain state-revisit controls separately.

### 15.3 Overaggressive state merging

**Failure:** two configurations with the same visible state but different excluded moves are merged.  
**Correction:** include future-relevant path context or prove a sound equivalence relation.

### 15.4 Permanent exclusion of temporary infeasibility

**Failure:** a move blocked by insufficient capacity is globally banned even after capacity changes.  
**Correction:** scope and version the infeasibility certificate.

### 15.5 Heuristic rejection mislabeled as proof

**Failure:** a low-scoring candidate is permanently pruned without a valid bound.  
**Correction:** represent heuristic filtering as deferment or explicitly incomplete pruning.

### 15.6 Version laundering

**Failure:** the generator assigns a fresh identifier to the same semantic operation on each visit, bypassing deduplication.  
**Correction:** canonicalize operation meaning and version only material context changes.

### 15.7 Unsound distributed exclusion

**Failure:** one worker's path-local No decision is broadcast as a global prohibition.  
**Correction:** require explicit decision scope and proof before global propagation.

### 15.8 Budget confused with proof

**Failure:** search stops at a time limit and reports that no solution exists.  
**Correction:** report `INCOMPLETE` or a bounded-quality result unless a valid proof is available.

## 16. Conformance-Oriented Test Matrix

The following are proposed minimum tests for the future SPEC-003 contract.

| Test ID | Scenario | Expected property |
|---|---|---|
| PM-001 | Accept candidate `d` and regenerate `d` on same constructive path | No second undecided resolution |
| PM-002 | Exclude candidate `d` and regenerate `d` on same path | NoSubtree exclusion persists |
| PM-003 | Exclude `d` in one sibling, accept in another | Sibling path isolation |
| PM-004 | Execute `a`, then `b`, returning to earlier state | State loop detected independently of Delta identity |
| PM-005 | Same visible state, different future-relevant exclusions | No unsound cache merge |
| PM-006 | Temporarily infeasible Delta becomes feasible after valid context change | Scoped invalidation or revalidation |
| PM-007 | Two different Deltas yield equivalent states | Sound equivalence handling or distinct records |
| PM-008 | One Delta yields multiple nondeterministic outcomes | One decision, multiple outcome edges supported |
| PM-009 | Heuristic filter removes a candidate | Completeness status reflects omitted exploration |
| PM-010 | Worker retry of same exploration contract | Idempotent result integration or explicit duplicate tagging |
| PM-011 | Global constraint update invalidates old cache entry | Version-safe cache behavior |
| PM-012 | Finite one-shot candidate universe of size `m` | Maximum decision depth at most `m` |
| PM-013 | Endless generation of semantically identical versioned Deltas | Canonical deduplication or explicit nontermination risk |
| PM-014 | Budget exhausted before frontier is empty | Status `INCOMPLETE`, not `UNSAT` |

Tests should assert both observable results and internal proof obligations (decision scope, cache key fields, reason codes) where feasible.

## 17. Testable Hypotheses and Experimental Program

**H1 — NoSubtree memory reduces duplicate exploration.** On fixed constructive assignment benchmarks, explicit rejection memory reduces repeated decision expansion compared with a baseline that records only accepted operations. Measure duplicate nodes, total expansions, runtime, and memory overhead.

**H2 — Scoped identities improve correctness under changing contexts.** Compared with unscoped global bans, context-aware exclusion preserves more valid reconfiguration solutions without introducing invalid transitions. Measure false-pruning rate and validation cost.

**H3 — State-level caching can outperform path-only deduplication when equivalence is sound.** Evaluate on graph-like search tasks, reporting cache hit rate, memory use, runtime, and solution completeness.

**H4 — Loop policy is problem-class dependent.** Compare forbid-revisit, dominance-based revisit, and budgeted revisit on constructive, reconfigurable, and cyclic domains. Expect no universal winner.

**H5 — Typed decision provenance improves distributed relay.** Compare workers exchanging only solutions against workers exchanging scoped decisions, proof references, and method evidence. Measure redundant work, correctness violations, communication overhead, and improvement on held-out tasks.

**H6 — Exploratory localization benefits from negative evidence.** In selected SIF-style localization tasks, test whether preserving exclusions and their reasons improves target discovery under equal budgets. Report failures and cases where memory becomes stale or harmful.

### 17.1 Benchmark design requirements

Use small exhaustive instances with known ground truth, larger practical instances, and adversarial examples specifically constructed to expose identity and loop defects. Include at least one reversible domain and one context-changing domain. Compare against standard graph-search and constraint-solving baselines, not only against an intentionally memoryless implementation. Separate correctness tests from performance claims.

## 20. Conclusion

CDE's generality depends on separating three concepts that a simple binary tree can easily conceal: the identity of a transformation, the resolution of a candidate decision, and the identity of a reachable state. In constructive BTP-style search, **Yes and No both consume a one-shot decision**; an exclusion is a real part of the path's information state. In more general CDE profiles, legitimate reconfiguration, cycles, and adaptive context changes require explicit alternatives to one-shot memory rules. Neither Delta uniqueness nor decision uniqueness alone prevents state loops, and neither loop detection nor a finite budget by itself establishes completeness or optimality.

The resulting principle is simple but powerful: **every realized branch carries its Delta and transition semantics; every path carries the decisions relevant to its future; every solver declares how equivalent states and revisits are governed.** With these contracts, CDE can support trees, DAGs, cyclic graphs, heterogeneous solvers, distributed workers, and evidence-driven Structural Relay without silently conflating their correctness conditions.
